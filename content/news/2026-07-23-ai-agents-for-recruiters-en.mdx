An AI agent for recruiters takes the repetitive top of the funnel — sourcing candidates, screening applications, scheduling interviews, and answering routine questions — so a recruiter spends time on people, not admin. What it should not do on its own is make the hire-or-reject call. That decision stays human, and in some cities the law requires it.

## The recruiter's funnel, and which parts an agent can take

Hiring is a pipeline: find candidates, rank them, talk to them, and decide. An AI agent is genuinely useful for the first three — they're high-volume, repetitive, and low-stakes if the agent is wrong, because a recruiter reviews the output before anyone gets hired or rejected. The fourth stage — the actual cut — is where a wrong call is expensive and, increasingly, regulated. So the honest way to read every "AI agent for recruiting" pitch is to ask, for each stage, *does the agent do the work, or make the decision?* Do the work: keep it. Make the decision: keep a human on it. It's the same sort-vs-act line we drew for [resume screening](/en/news/2026-07-22-ai-agent-for-resume-screening/) and for [lead qualification](/en/news/2026-07-18-ai-agent-for-lead-qualification/) — and the same bucket-then-automate discipline behind our [Gmail triage agent](/en/posts/2026-05-13-gmail-ai-agent/) in Issue #001.

## Source — find the candidates

The top of the funnel is search, and it's where agents save the most obvious time. [LinkedIn's Hiring Assistant](https://business.linkedin.com/hire/hiring-assistant) — described by LinkedIn as its first AI agent for recruiters — takes a role described in plain English, runs dozens of searches across LinkedIn to surface qualified candidates you might not reach, and adapts to your feedback over time. It [went generally available in late September 2025](https://news.linkedin.com/2025/hiring-assistant-globally-available), and LinkedIn says customers report saving an average of 4+ hours per user, per role. As [HR Dive reported](https://www.hrdive.com/news/linkedin-ai-agent-hiring-assistant/731402/), the same agent can also ask screening questions and prescreen candidates through InMail. The recruiter still picks who to pursue — the agent builds the pool, not the offer.

## Screen — rank the applicants

Once applications land, the job is ranking, not rejecting. [Workday](https://www.workday.com/en-us/topics/ai/ai-in-recruiting.html) frames AI in recruiting as analyzing applications at scale to identify the candidates who best match a role by skills and experience, so a recruiter reads the strongest matches instead of every application cold. That's the safe half — a ranked shortlist with reasons visible. The unsafe half is letting the agent auto-reject before a human looks, which is a different job with legal weight; we split it in full in the [resume-screening guide](/en/news/2026-07-22-ai-agent-for-resume-screening/). The rule of thumb: an agent that ranks and explains is a tool; one that filters people out silently is a decision-maker.

## Engage & schedule — book the interviews, answer the questions

The middle of the funnel is coordination, and it's the most thankless part of a recruiter's week. [Paradox's assistant Olivia](https://www.paradox.ai/products/conversational-scheduling) lets candidates self-schedule interviews over SMS, WhatsApp, chat, or email, syncs directly with hiring managers' calendars, handles multi-person and multi-location interviews, and sends automated reminders — the calendar Tetris a recruiter would otherwise do by hand. Paradox also has Olivia [screen candidates against job requirements](https://www.paradox.ai/products/screening) and answer routine candidate questions around the clock. This is the same coordinate-and-book job we mapped for [calendar scheduling](/en/news/2026-07-13-ai-agent-calendar-scheduling/), pointed at candidates instead of colleagues. Engagement is safe to automate precisely because it's logistics — no one is hired or rejected by a scheduling link.

## Decide — the one stage that stays human

Here's the line every recruiter has to hold: the moment an agent *rejects* or *selects* a candidate on its own, it stops being an assistant and becomes an **automated employment decision tool (AEDT)**. Under [New York City's Local Law 144](https://rules.cityofnewyork.us/rule/automated-employment-decision-tools-updated/), an employer may not use an AEDT to screen NYC candidates unless it has passed an independent bias audit within the past year, published a summary of the results, and given candidates advance notice. The [NYC Department of Consumer and Worker Protection's FAQ](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/dca/downloads/pdf/about/DCWP-AEDT-FAQ.pdf) confirms that scoring candidates and screening resumes fall inside the rule, with penalties for using an unaudited tool. Other jurisdictions are adding similar rules. The safe posture — and the honest one — is to let agents do the sourcing, ranking, and scheduling, and keep a person on every hire-or-reject decision.

## Which agent should a recruiter start with?

Start where your week actually hurts. If sourcing eats your hours, a sourcing agent like [LinkedIn's Hiring Assistant](https://business.linkedin.com/hire/hiring-assistant) is the highest-leverage first step. If it's the volume of applications, a screening layer that ranks (not rejects) is next — read the [screen-vs-decide breakdown](/en/news/2026-07-22-ai-agent-for-resume-screening/) before you turn anything on. If it's interview coordination, a scheduling agent like Olivia removes the back-and-forth. New to agents entirely? Whatever you start with, keep the same rule: automate the work, not the decision.

## FAQ

**What AI agents should a recruiter use?**
Match the agent to the funnel stage. For sourcing, [LinkedIn's Hiring Assistant](https://business.linkedin.com/hire/hiring-assistant) runs searches and surfaces candidates; for screening, [Workday's recruiting AI](https://www.workday.com/en-us/topics/ai/ai-in-recruiting.html) ranks applications at scale; for scheduling and candidate questions, [Paradox's Olivia](https://www.paradox.ai/products/conversational-scheduling) books interviews and answers around the clock. Start with the stage that costs you the most time.

**Can an AI agent do the whole hiring process?**
It can do most of the pipeline — sourcing, screening, outreach, scheduling — but you should not let it make the hire-or-reject call unsupervised. That final decision carries legal risk (see [Local Law 144](https://rules.cityofnewyork.us/rule/automated-employment-decision-tools-updated/)) and is exactly where human judgment matters most.

**Is it legal to let an AI agent reject candidates automatically?**
It's regulated, not free. In New York City, a tool that screens or scores candidates is an automated employment decision tool, and Local Law 144 requires an independent bias audit within the past year, a public results summary, and advance notice to candidates before use — the [DCWP FAQ](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/dca/downloads/pdf/about/DCWP-AEDT-FAQ.pdf) confirms resume screening is covered. Keep a human on the reject decision.

**How much time do recruiting agents actually save?**
LinkedIn says customers report saving an average of 4+ hours per user, per role with [Hiring Assistant](https://news.linkedin.com/2025/hiring-assistant-globally-available). The savings come from the repetitive stages — search, initial screening, scheduling — not from the agent making decisions, which is where you want to slow down, not speed up.

**Do I need code to use a recruiting agent?**
No. Hiring Assistant, Workday's recruiting AI, and Paradox's Olivia ship as configurable features inside the platforms recruiters already use.
