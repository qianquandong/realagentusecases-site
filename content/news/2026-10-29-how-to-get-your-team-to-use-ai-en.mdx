Getting a team to use AI is a behavior problem, not a licensing one — buying seats doesn't create habits. What works: start with one real task people already dislike, let a few early adopters prove it, wire it into the existing workflow, make experimentation safe, and measure real use instead of logins.

You can roll a tool out to everyone and still watch almost no one use it. That gap — between access and habit — is the hard part, and no purchase order closes it. [Rolling a tool out](/en/news/2026-10-20-microsoft-copilot-rollout-for-teams/) gets people a login; getting them to *use* it is a separate job, and it's the one most teams skip.

## Buying licenses is not adoption

The gap is real and measured. BCG's [AI at Work 2025](https://www.bcg.com/press/26june2025-beyond-ai-adoption-full-potential) survey of more than 10,600 workers across 11 countries found that while 72% now use AI regularly, only 36% feel adequately trained to use it — and its headline is that companies have to go *beyond adoption* to see the payoff. Microsoft's [2025 Work Trend Index](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/worklab/work-trend-index/2025-the-year-the-frontier-firm-is-born) tells the same story from inside the org: leaders are consistently further along than the employees they manage — more familiar with the tools, more convinced of the value. The people deciding to buy are not the people who have to change how they work.

So the first move is honest: a full license count is a cost, not a signal. [Seats are not adoption](/en/news/2026-10-22-how-to-measure-ai-adoption-at-work/) — the number that matters is how many people actually use the tool, and keep using it, after launch week.

## Start with one real task, not "use AI"

"Use AI" is not a task anyone can act on. The teams that get traction do the opposite of a broad mandate: they pick one concrete, recurring job the team already dislikes and show AI handling *that*. It's the same discipline behind every build on this site — [Issue #001](/en/posts/2026-05-13-gmail-ai-agent/) didn't start with "let's use AI on email," it started with a specific inbox that took too long to triage every morning, and a test of whether that one job got easier.

A real task gives people something they can feel. When someone watches their own weekly status report drafted in a tenth of the time, or a messy thread summarized before a meeting, the abstract case for AI becomes a concrete one they'll repeat tomorrow. One good task beats ten tool demos.

## Let a few people prove it — don't mandate

Adoption spreads through peers, not policy. Forcing use through a compliance email is the fastest way to get surface compliance and quiet resentment; what sticks is a handful of early adopters who try the tool on their real work and show colleagues what it did. Microsoft's own rollout guidance is built this way: its [Copilot Scenario Library](https://adoption.microsoft.com/copilot-scenario-library) sorts use cases by role and department so a champion can pick the one relevant to *their* team, and the [staged-pilot approach](/en/news/2026-10-20-microsoft-copilot-rollout-for-teams/) starts small and engaged before it goes wide.

Make experimentation safe while you're at it. People won't try a tool they're afraid of getting wrong, so say out loud that early outputs are expected to be rough, name which uses are approved, and keep the one hard rule explicit: AI drafts, a human still decides. That [human review gate](/en/news/2026-08-13-human-in-the-loop-ai-agents/) is what lets people experiment freely — the agent proposes, the person approves, so a bad draft is caught, not shipped.

## Wire it into the workflow they already have

A tool people have to remember to open is a tool they'll forget. The organizations seeing real impact in McKinsey's [State of AI](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-state-of-ai) reporting are the ones that redesign the workflow around AI rather than bolt it onto the side — the use has to live where the work already happens. If the weekly report lives in a specific doc, the AI help belongs in that doc, at that moment, not in a separate chat window someone has to think to visit.

Training is the other half, and a single launch session is not it. BCG found that the workers who became regular users were the ones who got more and better training — hands-on, with coaching — not one webinar. Skill with these tools builds through repetition on real work, so budget for practice, not just an announcement.

## Measure real use, then keep going

You can't manage what you won't look at. Once a task is landing, [measure adoption honestly](/en/news/2026-10-22-how-to-measure-ai-adoption-at-work/): active users against the seats you bought, retention over several weeks, and the one task-level outcome you baselined before launch. Rising active-users and a task that measurably got easier mean it's working; full seats with a flat usage rate mean you bought a license bill, not a habit. Then pick the next task and repeat — adoption is a sequence of small, proven wins, not a one-time switch.

## FAQ

**How do I get my team to actually use AI?**
Treat it as behavior change, not a license rollout. Pick one concrete, recurring task the team already dislikes and show AI handling it; let a few early adopters prove it on real work rather than mandating use from the top; make experimentation safe by saying rough early outputs are expected and keeping a human-approves-the-result rule; wire the help into the workflow where the work already happens; and measure active use, not logins. One proven task, repeated and spread by peers, beats a company-wide mandate.

**Why isn't my team using the AI tools we already bought?**
Because a license is access, not a habit — and access was never the hard part. The common causes are no concrete task (people were told to "use AI" with no job attached), no safe space to be bad at it, no one on the team modeling it, the tool living outside their real workflow, and a single training session that didn't stick. BCG's AI at Work 2025 found only 36% of workers feel adequately trained even as regular use hits 72%; the fix is usually a real task plus real practice, not a stronger email.

**Should I make AI use mandatory?**
A blanket mandate tends to produce surface compliance and resentment, not genuine adoption — people log in to satisfy the metric and go back to their old way. Set expectations and remove excuses instead: name approved use cases, give people one clear task to try, make the training real, and let early adopters pull the rest in. Reserve firm rules for the guardrails that matter — what data is off-limits and where a human must still approve — not for forcing the habit itself.

**Who should lead AI adoption on a team?**
Not IT alone. The pattern that works pairs leadership backing (budget, permission to spend time learning, a clear "this matters") with a few respected peers — champions — who use the tool on their own real work and show others. Microsoft's Copilot Scenario Library exists precisely so a champion can pick the use case relevant to their department rather than inherit a generic one. Managers who use it themselves, visibly, move a team far faster than a policy from above.

**How long does it take for a team to adopt AI?**
Think weeks to months, not a launch day. A launch-week spike measures curiosity; habit shows up as retention — people still using the tool several weeks later on real work. Set a baseline before you start, check active users and retention monthly, and expect adoption to climb one proven task at a time. If the numbers are flat after a few weeks, the problem is usually the task, the workflow fit, or the training — not the people.
