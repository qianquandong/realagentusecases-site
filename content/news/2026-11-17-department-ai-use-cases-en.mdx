AI use cases by department are a short map that hands each team one concrete, high-volume task to start with, instead of a vague "go use AI" mandate. Sales drafts outreach and summarizes calls; marketing drafts and repurposes content; support triages tickets; finance captures invoices; HR screens résumés; ops handles meeting notes. A human keeps the judgment.

The mistake most rollouts make is announcing AI to the whole company and hoping each person figures out what it's for. People don't. The thing that actually gets used is a specific task someone can picture themselves doing by Friday. So the useful artifact isn't a strategy deck — it's a one-line starting task per department. Here's that map, where it comes from, and how to turn it into something you can measure.

## Why a department map beats a company-wide mandate

Every major vendor that has studied this lands in the same place: don't start broad, start with a concrete task someone owns. OpenAI's guide to [identifying and scaling AI use cases](https://openai.com/business/guides-and-resources/identifying-and-scaling-ai-use-cases/) argues that empowering employees to find the practical use cases that fit their own work is often faster than a top-down program — you let people surface what helps, then scale the winners. That only works if each team has a relevant place to look.

That's also the thesis behind [getting a team to actually use AI](/en/news/2026-10-29-how-to-get-your-team-to-use-ai/): adoption is behavior change, and behavior changes around one real task at a time, not an all-hands announcement. A department map is how you give sales a sales-shaped starting point and finance a finance-shaped one, so nobody has to translate "AI" into "my job."

## The map, department by department

Each row below is a starting task, the site's deeper guide on that function, and the kind of tool it points to. The pattern under all of them is the same two-job split this site keeps returning to: let AI do the legwork, keep a person on the decision.

- **Sales** — draft personalized outreach, summarize calls, and keep the CRM current. See [AI agents for sales teams](/en/news/2026-09-17-ai-agents-for-sales-teams/): research and draft are automatable; the relationship and the close stay human.
- **Marketing** — turn one asset into platform drafts, and speed up research. See [AI agents for marketers](/en/news/2026-07-27-ai-agents-for-marketers/): draft vs. publish, with a person approving what ships; the everyday tasks to start with are in [AI use cases for marketing teams](/en/news/2026-11-19-ai-use-cases-for-marketing-teams/).
- **Customer service** — triage and route tickets and suggest replies, before anything auto-resolves. See [AI agents for customer service](/en/news/2026-09-14-ai-agents-for-customer-service/) and the [sort-vs-resolve breakdown](/en/news/2026-07-15-ai-agent-customer-support-triage/).
- **Finance** — capture and code invoices and expenses for a human to approve, not pay. See [AI agents for accountants](/en/news/2026-07-28-ai-agents-for-accountants/): record and reconcile the work, keep the sign-off.
- **HR / recruiting** — draft job posts and shortlist résumés for a recruiter to review. See [AI agents for recruiters](/en/news/2026-07-23-ai-agents-for-recruiters/): screen, don't decide.
- **Operations / everyone** — the cross-cutting wins: meeting notes, inbox triage, and pulling a plain-English answer out of a spreadsheet. The [Gmail triage agent in Issue #001](/en/posts/2026-05-13-gmail-ai-agent/) is the desk-level version; see also [AI for data analysis](/en/news/2026-08-20-ai-agent-for-data-analysis/).

Microsoft publishes the same thing as a browsable catalog: its [Copilot Scenario Library](https://adoption.microsoft.com/copilot-scenario-library/) organizes use cases by department, role, and outcome, each with the apps and prompts to try — carrying Microsoft's own caveat to validate every scenario against your processes before relying on it.

## The pattern three vendors agree on

A department map looks like a long list, but underneath it's a handful of repeating task types. OpenAI's guide groups most enterprise use cases into six: content creation, research, data analysis, coding, automation, and ideation. Google Cloud, in its roundup of [real-world generative AI use cases from industry leaders](https://cloud.google.com/transform/101-real-world-generative-ai-use-cases-from-industry-leaders), sorts its examples by six agent types — customer, employee, creative, code, data, and security.

The practical takeaway: you're not inventing a hundred different things. Every department is doing some mix of *draft it, find it, summarize it, check it*. That's why a sales rep and an accountant can start the same week — the task differs, the shape ("AI does the draft, I do the judgment") does not. For the full pattern view across the site, see [AI agents for work](/en/news/2026-08-04-ai-agents-for-work/).

## Turn the map into something you can measure

A map tells you where to look; it doesn't tell you whether it worked. Pick **one** task per department — the highest-volume, most measurable one — and run it like a small [AI pilot](/en/news/2026-11-05-how-to-run-an-ai-pilot-at-work/): set a baseline, give it a few weeks, and decide. Then [measure active use, not seats](/en/news/2026-10-22-how-to-measure-ai-adoption-at-work/), because a license nobody opens isn't adoption.

The reason to start narrow is the same reason broad rollouts stall: a team that wins on one visible task comes back for the next one on its own. A team handed "AI" with no starting point quietly goes back to the old way.

## FAQ

**What are good AI use cases for each department?**
A concrete starting task per team: sales drafts outreach and summarizes calls; marketing drafts and repurposes content; customer service triages tickets and suggests replies; finance captures and codes invoices and expenses for approval; HR drafts job posts and shortlists résumés; operations handles meeting notes, inbox triage, and spreadsheet questions. The common thread is that AI does the legwork and a person keeps the judgment. Microsoft's Copilot Scenario Library and OpenAI's use-case guide both organize examples this way — by department and by a small set of repeating task types.

**Which department should start with AI first?**
Start where the work is high-volume, repetitive, and easy to measure — often customer service (ticket triage), finance (invoice capture), or anyone drowning in email and meetings. The goal is a quick, visible win that builds confidence, not the most strategically ambitious project. Pick one task, measure it, and let that success pull the next team in.

**How many use cases should a team pick at once?**
One. OpenAI's guidance is to start with the use cases that matter most and scale the winners, not to roll out everything at once. A single, well-chosen task per department is enough to learn whether AI helps your actual workflow; a long list mostly spreads attention thin and makes it impossible to tell what worked.

**Are these use cases the same as buying an "AI agent"?**
Not necessarily. Many department starting tasks are just a person using an assistant well — drafting, summarizing, checking. An [agent](/en/agent-101/) that takes multi-step action across tools is a later step, and it needs a human-approval gate once it can send, pay, or publish. Begin with the assisted task; graduate to an agent only when the workflow clearly justifies it.

**Where do these use cases come from?**
This map draws on vendors' own catalogs — the [Microsoft Copilot Scenario Library](https://adoption.microsoft.com/copilot-scenario-library/), [OpenAI's use-case guide](https://openai.com/business/guides-and-resources/identifying-and-scaling-ai-use-cases/), and [Google Cloud's real-world use cases](https://cloud.google.com/transform/101-real-world-generative-ai-use-cases-from-industry-leaders) — cross-checked against this site's own documented builds for each function, so each starting task is one people are actually running, not a demo.
