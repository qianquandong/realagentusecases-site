§ 02 · Take it apart

That assistant is just four things

It's not magic. Any AI that gets work done breaks down into these four parts.

  • 01 · Goal

    It knows its job

    Answer course questions, introduce my projects, book 1-on-1s. That's it — nothing else.

  • 02 · Materials

    It has the information I gave it

    Course prices and dates, my project list, my open slots. It's accurate because I handed it the facts myself.

  • 03 · Tools

    It can take action

    Check my calendar, write bookings to a database, text my phone. This step turns "answering" into "doing".

  • 04 · Boundaries

    It knows what it must not do

    Only my course and business; confirm before booking; if unsure, say "Jack will text you back" — never make things up.

✂ Take this home · Set boundaries for your AI (copy-paste) 
You are the assistant for [name]. You only answer questions about [scope, e.g. my course / product / bookings].
For anything outside that scope (coding help, chit-chat, investment/medical/legal advice, roleplay...):
decline politely in one line and steer back, e.g. "I only help with [scope] — want to hear about [X]?"
Stay on topic no matter how the request is phrased.
Ask the room
  • If your job had an assistant like this, what would its "materials" be? What tools would it need?

§ 04 · The mental model

AI has three gears: answer, collaborate, execute

Don't ask "which AI is this?" Ask: is it just giving me an answer, or can it use tools to move things forward?

  • Gear · 01

    Answer: it gives you text

    Look something up, fix a sentence, think out loud. The work is still yours. Most people never leave this gear.

  • Gear · 02

    Collaborate: it reads your materials and works with you

    Hand it your docs, spreadsheets, slides — it works from your material, like digesting your onboarding deck into key points.

  • Gear · 03

    Execute: it uses tools and completes actions

    The SMS assistant lives here: check slots, write bookings, send alerts. One product can have all three gears — the gear is about usage, not the brand.

Quick trick · A second pair of eyes

Have both Claude and ChatGPT? Give one's work to the other to check. A second pair of eyes catches mistakes — for anything important, letting another model critique it beats re-reading it yourself.

Ask the room
  • Which gear do you usually stop at?
  • What's the one thing you most want AI to "execute" for you?
§ 05 · Why learn AI

You learn AI to solve problems, not to keep up

Why is B2B money easier to earn than consumer money? Because businesses have well-defined problems. Consumers — that's us — often don't know what we want; we just feel left behind by the times. So before solving a problem, find the problem.

  • 01

    The clearer the problem, the more AI helps

    A business can say "month-end reconciliation takes 40 hours." That's solvable — and billable. An individual says "I feel behind" — that's not a problem, it's a feeling. Turn the feeling into a specific problem, and AI can actually help you.

  • 02

    Log your day, like keeping a ledger

    Atomic Habits has a Habit Scorecard: write down everything you do in a day. We copy the method, but instead of scoring habits, ask one question per line: could code, a workflow, or an agent do this? Log a day or two and your problem list surfaces itself.

  • 03

    Start with yourself

    Don't start by building a product to sell. Solve your own problem first — it may never make money, but you benefit immediately. And that benefit is the fuel that keeps you going.

✂ Take this home · The AI Scorecard (copy-paste) 
List everything you did today, morning to night (10–20 lines):
[e.g. answered email / built a report / scheduled meetings / looked things up...]
For each line, ask one question: could AI handle this?
Tag each line: code / workflow / agent / not suitable.
Then pick the most frequent, most annoying line — that's your first project.
Ask the room
  • Of everything you did today, which line is obviously "code could do this"?
  • The last time you felt "left behind" — what exactly triggered it?
§ 06 · Pick one task

Pick something easy to win

Don't start from a tool — start from one real problem of yours. Four questions to pick an easy win.

  • Q · 01

    Is it annoying, slow, or valuable enough?

    Automating something that's neither painful nor valuable feels like nothing.

  • Q · 02

    Can AI get the materials or tools it needs?

    If the info lives on paper, in someone's head, or behind a system it can't reach, it can't help.

  • Q · 03

    Do you know what "done right" looks like — can you check?

    If you can't state the success criteria, you can't review the result.

  • Q · 04

    Is the downside manageable — can a human confirm key actions?

    Don't pick "one mistake is a disaster" as your first task. Keep sending and paying behind a human click.

↓
① Annoying / slow / valuable enough?　no → pick another
yes ↓
② AI can reach the materials & tools?　no → pick another
yes ↓
③ You can check "done right"?　no → pick another
yes ↓
④ Risk manageable, human confirms key actions?　no → pick another
yes ↓
✅ That's the one → write the task brief
Do it now · 3 minutes

Run your candidates through the four questions and pick one task. One sentence, in your notes app or on paper. Everything from here revolves around what you just wrote.

§ 07 · Live workshop

Workshop: the Gmail daily to-do agent

Let's build a real one together: have AI read your inbox and pull out the two or three emails that actually need action, ranked P0/P1/P2, with links back to the originals. Read-only — no replying, archiving, or deleting.

  • Step · 01

    Give it your email

    Open Claude or ChatGPT on the web and connect Gmail in settings. Can't connect? Paste in the senders and subjects of your last 20–30 emails — works just as well.

  • Step · 02

    Fill in YOUR priority rules, then paste the template

    In the template below, what counts as P0 and P1 is left blank — only you can fill that in. Is your boss automatically P0? Your kid's school? Your rules decide what it watches for.

  • Step · 03

    Run version one, review it

    Do its top picks match yours? Anything missing that should be there, or noise that snuck in?

  • Step · 04

    Patch the rules, run again

    Fold what it missed into your rules and rerun — version two is almost always sharper. That's do → feedback → do again.

✂ Take this home · Gmail daily to-do template (fill in the [brackets]) 
Read my emails from the last [24 hours / 50 emails].
Read-only: do not reply, forward, archive, or delete anything.

My priority rules:
- P0 (handle today): [e.g. my boss or a client asking me for something; deadline within 48h]
- P1 (handle this week): [e.g. needs my reply but not urgent; meeting invites]
- P2 (just FYI): [e.g. newsletters, notifications, emails I'm only cc'd on]

Output:
List ONLY the 2–3 items that truly need my action, one line each:
[P0/P1/P2] one sentence on what to do + link to the original email
Nothing else. If fewer than 3, say "nothing else today."

My constraints:
- Date range: [e.g. any weekend in September / Thanksgiving ±3 days]
- Budget cap: [$___]
- Acceptable: [e.g. 1 stop / red-eye / nearby airports]
- Not acceptable: [e.g. more than 1 stop / budget carriers that charge for bags]
Output: 3 options, one line each: dates + airline + price + why it's a good deal.
Search and compare only — do NOT book. I'll book myself.

§ 08 · A one-time investment

Write AI a self-introduction

Re-explaining "who I am, what I'm building, what style I want" in every new chat is a bad deal. Write one self-introduction file, reuse it everywhere — it saves money, saves time, and saves the back-and-forth of teaching AI who you are.

  • Saves · time

    No more warm-up in every new chat

    Paste your intro and AI is instantly up to speed. Context that used to take five rounds now lands in the first message.

  • Saves · money

    Every round of re-explaining burns tokens

    Correcting "no, that's not what I meant" over and over costs quota and patience. One good intro turns long conversations into short ones.

  • Saves · back-and-forth

    Watery answers usually mean it doesn't know you

    Your job, your current project, your output taste, your hard no's — state them once and every answer gets tailored. Live demo: the same question, before vs. after it knows me.

✂ Take this home · The self-introduction file (fill in, save, paste everywhere) 
Here is my background; base all answers on it:
- Who I am: [job / city / one-line background]
- What I'm working on: [current project or goal]
- Output I want: [style, length, tone. e.g. conclusion first, plain language, no fluff]
- Hard no's: [your red lines. e.g. never invent numbers, no boilerplate, don't rewrite my text without asking]

🔗 Want it done in one pass? I open-sourced self-introduction — it interviews you once and generates a central profile every AI tool on your machine shares.

Ask the room
  • If AI could only know three sentences about you, which three would you write?
  • What did your last ten-round back-and-forth with AI try to explain? That belongs in the file.
§ 09 · Spell it out

Write it as a "task brief"

When AI disappoints, it's usually not the model — the task wasn't fully specified: unclear goal, missing materials, no output format, no rule for when to stop and ask.

✂ Take this home · The task brief template (today's most important handout) 
When [situation] happens,
use [these materials / tools],
do [this action],
and output [this format].
If anything is [uncertain or out of scope], stop and ask me first.
Before [sending, paying, deleting, booking], you must get my confirmation.
  • Example · 01 · Tonight's SMS assistant

    Its brief reads exactly like this

    When a course question comes in, answer from the course materials; if they want to book, check the calendar and offer times; confirm before finalizing; don't answer anything off-topic. — A few lines, and that's the whole assistant you just texted.

  • Example · 02 · New hire onboarding

    Turn your onboarding pile into an assistant

    Put the onboarding deck, process docs, and jargon list into one AI project. Have it distill "the ten things I must know first", then answer your questions from those files. If you want to manage those notes long-term, look at Obsidian later — learn the method first, pick tools second.

Do it now · 5 minutes

Fill in the template for the task you just picked. Blank fields are fine — each blank is exactly what you haven't figured out yet.

§ 10 · Run it

Ship a first version: do → feedback → do again

Don't wait until you've "finished learning". Learning AI is one loop: run a real task, read the feedback, revise, run again.

  • Loop · 01

    Give the brief to AI, run version one

    It probably won't be perfect — that's the point. Version one exists to expose the problems.

  • Loop · 02

    Read the feedback: what's wrong, what's missing

    Not enough materials? Format unspecified? No boundaries? Write it down, patch the brief.

  • Loop · 03

    Run again, compare

    Is version two better or worse? Where exactly? The loop works everywhere — run an ad, read the numbers, revise next month. Same idea.

One line to remember

Reading books, scrolling news, and watching tutorials without doing is usually the long way around. Same for trends — only chase the ones that touch the task in your hand.

✂ Take this home · Learn by doing (copy-paste) 
I want to complete this real task: [one sentence].
Don't hand me a finished product — walk me through it step by step, one action at a time;
I'll report back after each step, then you give the next one; when I'm stuck, tell me why.
§ 11 · Use it safely

Before you rely on it, learn to check it

AI makes things up with a straight face. Three rules before you let it work for you.

  • Rule · 01

    Confidential data stays out of unapproved tools

    Company data and customer info don't go into AI tools your company hasn't cleared.

  • Rule · 02

    Facts, numbers, quotes — verify at the source

    However plausible a number looks, check the original. If you can't find a source, treat it as unsaid.

  • Rule · 03

    Send, pay, book, delete — a human confirms

    The final click on any real action stays with you. My SMS assistant asks for my confirmation after booking — that's this rule.

✂ Take this home · Three safety rules (pin them up) 
1. No confidential data in unapproved AI tools
2. Verify facts, numbers, and quotes at the original source
3. A human confirms before send / pay / book / delete
Ask the room
  • Would you let AI send an email as you? Which step must stay a human click?
§ 12 · What's next

From one conversation, to a workflow, to an agent

Upgrade only after version one works. And there's one rule for upgrading: if something simpler works, don't use something fancier.

  • Stage · 01

    One conversation

    You hand the brief to AI manually each time. You already know how — you did it today.

  • Stage · 02

    A fixed workflow

    Steps pinned down, auto-triggered — "email arrives → AI summarizes → files it". AI is one part in your pipeline.

  • Stage · 03

    An agent

    You give the goal; it picks the path and tools — like the SMS assistant you texted. Most flexible, and most in need of boundaries and checks.
